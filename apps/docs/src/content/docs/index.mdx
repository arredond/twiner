**TWIN-ER** is a digital twin for natural-hazard risk in Spain. Pick a hazard
scenario on a map, and it estimates what that scenario would do to every
building in its path. It then rolls those estimates up into reports per
municipality and per census section: how many buildings are damaged, how
many people live in them, and what the response would need.

It covers all of Spain, about 13 million cadastral buildings in more than
8,100 municipalities. Everything is built from open, official data. The
design is not specific to Spain: each country-specific dataset sits behind a
pipeline that can be swapped for another country's equivalent.

TWIN-ER started as a modern rebuild of
[MERISUR](#background), a seismic scenario simulator for the town of Lorca
built by the Universidad Politécnica de Madrid (UPM). It then grew in three
directions: from one town to the whole country, from one hazard to several,
and from a research tool towards an operational one.

Both hazards share the same exposure model, the same reporting units and the
same API. Live layers sit alongside them: traffic incidents from Spain's
traffic authorities, and observations and warnings from the state weather
agency (AEMET). They show what is happening now next to what a scenario
predicts.

## What a scenario produces

Every scenario answers at three levels of detail:

- **Buildings.** For an earthquake, each building's most likely damage
  state (None, Slight, Moderate, Extensive or Complete) and the full
  probability distribution behind it. For a flood, whether its footprint
  lies in the flood zone. On the map, damaged buildings also show a
  **debris envelope**: the strip of street their rubble could reach.
- **Census sections and municipalities.** The reporting units of Spain's
  National Statistics Institute (INE): 36,554 census sections nested in
  municipalities, provinces and autonomous communities (CCAA). For each one:
  - buildings affected, in number and as a % of the area's stock;
  - resident population affected, and how many of them are **vulnerable**
    (under 15 or 65 and over);
  - for earthquakes: residents **displaced** (living in buildings with
    Extensive or Complete damage), material repair **cost**, **debris**
    weight, the number of **truck trips** to clear it, and the **shoring
    props** needed;
  - for floods: dwellings and area inside the flood zone.
- **Critical infrastructure.** Hospitals, health centres, schools,
  universities, care homes, police and emergency services, power plants and
  substations, bridges and dams, each with its estimated shaking intensity
  (EMS-98 scale) or flood exposure. Facilities also carry the damage state
  of the building they occupy.

These figures are **orders of magnitude for comparing places and
scenarios**, not calibrated loss predictions. Each page states its
assumptions and limitations. The
[impact estimates](/docs/impact-estimates/) page gives the exact formulas.

## Architecture

A handful of principles shape the technical design.

**Open source, open data.** Every input is published by a public body under
an open licence: the cadastre, the national geographic and statistics
institutes, the geological survey, the environment ministry, and European
research consortia. The scientific components are established, peer-reviewed
models with open implementations. Any result can be traced back to its
inputs and reproduced. See [data sources](/docs/data-sources/).

**Model-agnostic and modular.** Each step of a hazard chain is a separate,
swappable stage with a narrow interface. For an earthquake those stages are
the rupture, the ground-motion model, the site amplification, the
vulnerability classes, the fragility functions and the impact model.
Changing the ground-motion model or adding a set of fragility curves doesn't
touch the rest. A new hazard reuses the exposure model, the census and
infrastructure layers, the reporting units and the API: twinFLOOD was built
this way on top of twinQUAKE's foundations.

**Precompute what doesn't depend on the scenario.** Building footprints,
vulnerability classes, site conditions, census assignments, debris
envelopes and flood-zone membership are all properties of the place, not of
the event. Offline **pipelines** compute them once, for the whole country.
At request time a scenario only computes what genuinely depends on it, such
as the ground motion and the resulting damage. It then joins those results
onto data that already exists.

**Query static files in cloud-native formats.** The precomputed data is
stored as plain files in object storage, laid out so they can be read in
small pieces over HTTP range requests rather than through a database
server:

- **GeoParquet**, sorted spatially, so a query for one region reads only
  the row groups that overlap it. It is queried with **DuckDB** straight
  from object storage.
- **PMTiles**, single-file vector-tile archives that the browser reads
  directly: buildings, census sections, municipalities, flood zones,
  infrastructure, and a self-hosted OpenStreetMap basemap.

**Serverless and thin.** The API runs as short-lived serverless functions
(AWS Lambda), so there is nothing to keep running between scenarios. A
scenario's response holds only aggregates. Per-building results are joined
into the static building tiles one tile at a time as the map requests them,
so the browser never downloads a list of millions of buildings. Results are
stored under a content-addressed id: the same scenario on the same data
version is computed once and then served from storage.

## Background

MERISUR (*Metodología para la Evaluación Efectiva del Riesgo Sísmico
Urbano*) was a research project of UPM's Earthquake Engineering Research
Group. It produced a web simulator of earthquake scenarios for Lorca
(Murcia), the town hit by the damaging Mw 5.2 earthquake of 11 May 2011
[1, 2]. TWIN-ER keeps MERISUR's scenario logic: an automatic mode driven by
the active-fault database, a manual mode, the Akkar et al. (2014)
ground-motion model, and three probability levels. Where Lorca-specific
inputs (field surveys, a local soil microzonation, capacity curves) can't
extend to the whole country, it replaces them with national open datasets.
The [twinQUAKE](/docs/hazards/earthquake/) page sets out each of these
substitutions.
